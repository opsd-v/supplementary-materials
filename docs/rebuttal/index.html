What role does real history play in OPSD-V’s improvements?

Attention sink

The review leaves the term “more attention sinks” undefined, offering no concrete hypothesis or testable prediction and leaving us to infer what is being asked.

What is an attention sink? StreamingLLM demonstrated that initial tokens can attract disproportionately high attention in pretrained language models, a phenomenon that extends to causal video generation. LongLive showed that after streaming long tuning, retaining the first chunk as an attention sink improves long-range consistency. Subsequent chunks attend to this first chunk throughout the rollout; because it is generated before autoregressive errors accumulate and its cached content remains fixed during inference, it serves as a stable anchor for long video generation. Rolling Forcing similarly retains the KV of the first chunk (three latent frames) as a persistent anchor, while Reward Forcing extends this with EMA-Sink, which maintains a dedicated sink updated via exponential moving average. Training thus biases the model toward attending to this persistent initial reference. (StreamingLLM, LongLive, Rolling Forcing, Reward Forcing)

In contrast, OPSD-V directly overwrites an older slot in the teacher’s KV cache with features from the corresponding ground-truth (GT) video chunk. This replacement rule involves no learnable parameters, and the GT content changes dynamically as the rollout progresses. Consequently, the replaced slot does not function as an attention sink. Moreover, at inference time, the model uses only a single attention sink (the first chunk). For a strictly fair comparison, we apply this identical single-sink configuration across both LongLive and Self-Forcing, for the base models and OPSD-V alike. Multiple sinks therefore play no role in our results.

How real history shapes the teacher’s predictions

We initialized both branches with the original LongLive weights and queried them at the exact same student denoising states, where the teacher used real history in its cache while the student advanced its own rollout trajectory. Across 50 training videos sampled proportionally by data source, the mean student–teacher velocity MSE was consistently higher after 40 seconds than during the first 20 seconds across all four denoising steps. In the linked video examples, both decoded predictions follow the same scene progression, while the teacher preserves sharper visual details as the student’s rollout degrades. This demonstrates how the teacher provides an effective corrective target directly at the states the student visits.

How does a native autoregressive teacher compare?

The reviewer’s proposed substitution conflates on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) with on-policy distillation (OPD) from an external teacher—two fundamentally distinct formulations. Specifically, OPSD-V matches the velocity predictions of an EMA copy of the same four-step model at the student’s four fixed denoising states. By contrast, Causal Forcing’s teacher-forcing checkpoint is a multi-step AR diffusion model. While it can predict a velocity at each of those states, pointwise velocity matching does not explicitly match the trajectory transitions of the teacher’s multi-step sampler. Causal Forcing itself reports abrupt transitions when this checkpoint is sampled directly in four steps, requiring causal ODE distillation to adapt it to few-step generation (Causal Forcing, Appendix C.2 and Fig. 7).

Distilling a separate multi-step teacher is a fundamentally different problem: for instance, π-Flow uses intermediate integration substeps to imitate the teacher’s trajectory, and DiffusionOPD formulates an explicit transition-matching objective along the student’s rollout. Applying either approach would require an entirely different distillation formulation from OPSD-V (π-Flow · DiffusionOPD).

Although we anticipated this direct substitution would degrade performance, we implemented the reviewer’s suggestion and trained a student using Causal Forcing’s multi-step teacher-forced model as the teacher. Both the student and teacher shared the same real first chunk. Following OPSD-V, we queried the teacher at the student’s four denoising states and matched their velocity predictions via MSE. Under matched student inference settings, the resulting student degraded severely in both quantitative metrics and visual quality. On our benchmarks, we evaluated frame quality using MUSIQ (the no-reference metric underlying VBench’s Imaging Quality score). Over the first 5 seconds, the student trained with the multi-step teacher scored 0.2471, compared to 0.6979 for OPSD-V. Over the final 21 chunks, it scored 0.1867, compared to 0.6734 for OPSD-V. Note that this experiment simultaneously alters the teacher architecture, its guidance mechanism, and its history construction.

Beyond the choice of teacher model, we also ablated two alternative teacher-cache designs: 50% GT, which populates the older history chunk with ground-truth features with probability 0.5, and All-real, which additionally replaces the latest completed chunk with real-video features. Both ablations are detailed in our responses to Reviewers 7yiM and cGMm. 50% GT and All-real comparison · All-real full metrics

Reviewer 7yiM

How sensitive is OPSD-V to the proportion of real history?

We thank the reviewer for raising this question. The teacher’s attention window spans 12 latent frames divided into four three-frame chunks: the fixed first-chunk sink, one older history chunk, the most recently completed chunk, and the chunk currently being denoised. To evaluate how replacing generated history with time-aligned ground-truth (GT) KV affects performance, we tested two alternative policies alongside our default design:

  • 100% GT (Ours): Always replaces the older history chunk with time-aligned GT KV whenever available, while retaining the teacher’s own latest generated chunk.
  • 50% GT: Replaces the older history chunk with probability 0.5 when available, while still retaining the teacher’s latest generated chunk.
  • All-real: Overwrites every generated chunk’s KV with GT KV immediately upon completion, meaning both recent history slots contain real-video features.

We evaluated all three policies on LongLive. Our original 100% GT policy achieved the highest imaging quality over the final 21 chunks (0.7080). Lowering the replacement probability to 50% reduced the score to 0.6994, likely because more frequent replacement provides the teacher with cleaner real-video context, curbs error accumulation in its KV cache, and stabilizes its velocity targets. All-real scored the lowest at 0.6193: replacing the teacher’s latest generated chunk disrupts temporal continuity across chunk boundaries, degrading the reliability of the teacher’s supervision. We observed this exact boundary artifact in a training-free inference diagnostic applying the All-real policy to the original LongLive model (Appendix C.5, Fig. 7). In the motorcyclist example, overwriting the latest chunk’s KV forces the next chunk to attend to ground-truth frames that diverge from the preceding generated frames, causing abrupt shifts in the rider’s pose and background at the chunk boundary.

While exploring intermediate replacement probabilities between 50% and 100% remains interesting future work, our results on LongLive clearly favor consistently refreshing the older history slot with time-aligned GT while retaining the teacher’s latest generated chunk. The next section details how teacher predictions stay aligned with the student’s trajectory during training while exhibiting less visual degradation over long rollouts.

How are the teacher and student aligned?

The teacher and student share the same real first chunk as their attention sink, anchoring both branches to an identical initial scene. At the same time, each branch retains its own most recently generated chunk in its KV cache to preserve local temporal continuity from one chunk to the next.

OPSD-V keeps supervision strictly on-policy by evaluating both branches on the exact same noisy student latent at the same denoising timestep:

  • Step 1: Both branches receive the identical initial Gaussian noise for the current chunk.
  • Steps 2–4: Both branches receive the noisy latent obtained by applying the sampler transition to the student’s clean prediction from the preceding step.

The teacher evaluates this shared latent using its own KV cache (with the older slot refreshed from real video), while the student alone determines the denoising trajectory of the current chunk.

The diagnostic on the linked page reproduces these exact training-time inputs using the original LongLive weights. Across these examples, the student and teacher predictions follow the same scene progression, while the teacher preserves sharper details and exhibits fewer artifacts in later chunks.
